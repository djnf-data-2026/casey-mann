Examining the incentives behind Arkansas’s rural prison builds

Author

Casey Mann

Published

May 29, 2026

Arkansas prisons and their host counties

This investigation examines whether Arkansas counties that host state prisons experience measurable economic benefits compared to similar-sized counties without prisons, using Census income and population data to test the assumption that prison construction improves local economies. The broader goal is to examine financial incentives that arise as a result of how the U.S. counts prison populations.

Why now?

To much debate from residents and lawmakers alike, Arkansas Governor Sarah Huckabee Sanders announced plans for the construction of a 3,000 bed prison in rural Franklin County in October 2024. Two years later, construction is stalled, but it is unclear what effect the facility could have on it’s host county.

When plans for the facility were released by the Arkansas Department of Corrections in 2024, the project was framed as an economic opportunity for Franklin County. The build’s information sheet includes a “Community Benefits” section that details several financial incentives for residents, including:

  • Investing hundreds of millions of dollars in construction creates thousands of jobs.
  • Permanent, recession-proof employment for up too 800 individuals with annual salaries greater than $46,00.
  • Increased tax base to be invested in better public services, schools, and parks.

google

These incentives may sound promising for those in historically economically disadvantaged and under-funded counties. However, they raise a question about whether these benefits are truly actualized in counties that host state prisons.

This investigation uses Census income data from the nine Arkansas counties that currently house state facilities to examine whether these rural counties truly see long-term benefits as a result of hosting a facility.

Previous coverage

Coverage of the Franklin County prison has been largely dominated by details of the cost of the construction itself, but I cannot seem to find much detail on any potential long-term impacts of the build. Much of the coverage contains dissenting opinions from Franklin County residents, who often express concern that the facility could lower quality of life and property values. The potential build gained national attention when Franklin County Sheriff Johnny Crocker began to criticize the plans, saying several Arkansas counties requested the build, and Franklin was not one of them. He also said the United States Immigration and Customers Enforcement was considering using the proposed prison site as a detention facility.

Target Audience

This project aims to serve both rural Arkansans and policy makers by outlining the impact that hosting a prison can have on a community and therefore assisting in decision making.

Potential Interviews

  1. Business owners in Franklin County. Do they feel like the facility could push industry or customers away?
  2. Franklin County residents. How do they feel about the potential build? Does the presence of the proposed facility change how they feel about their home? What about their plans for the future? Will anyone want to move if construction is resumed or completed?
  3. Residents of Malvern, Arkansas, home to the most recent prison build in the state. Despite occurring more than 20 years ago, some residents may still recall how the facility changed the area. Potential for solid anecdotal details.
  4. Lawmakers on both sides of the argument for the facility’s construction.
    • FOR: Republican Sen. Jonathan Dismang of Searcy, who filed an appropriations bill during the 2025 session that would have provided $750 million in spending authority for the prison project. google
    • AGAINST: Republican Sen. Bryan King of Green Forest, who has been an outspoken critic of plans to construct the Franklin County facility. google
  5. Franklin County Sheriff Johnny Crocker, who has expressed frustration with the build.

Potential Impact

This story has the potential to impact both Franklin County residents and Arkansas policy. Residents who may feel like they are in the dark about what is happening in their own community could get access to data-backed context that informs decisions about whether they support the build. The story also serves to provide insight into how Franklin could be affected long-term if policymakers follow through with the build. This project could also provide insight into who benefits from continued incarceration, potentially influencing opinions about continued facility expansion efforts.

Arkansas policymakers could get an empirical look at how the Franklin County facility will change the lives of those who live in the area, potentially affecting how they vote on the issue in a moment when the future of the project appears to be up in the air.

The per capita income of prison counties and the median income of prison counties are both lower than their statewide counterparts. This may serve as an argument against the idea that building a prison in a rural, impoverished county improves the economy of the region and the lives of those who live there.

However, it is a possibility that these prison counties may fall below state averages because prison builds are intentionally placed in already rural, impoverished communities. To accurately assess the economy of these prison counties, we must compare their median income to counties of similar size.

Throughout the construction of this project, I came to realize that another factor to consider when discussing the impact of a new prison build in a rural county is the amount of additional federal funding the facility could provide. When the U.S. Census is conducted, inmates are counted as residents of the prison county they reside in, despite the fact that they are often not from the area and did not choose to be there. These inmates, who are often from majority Black and Latino, urban communities, boost the population metrics of the majority white, rural counties they are held in. These prison counties are then, in turn, eligible for more federal funding. As a result of the population increase from inmates, free people in these counties may benefit off the incarceration of others. This is known as prison gerrymandering.
